# Food delivery, without the cut

*Focused scan, November 2026. Sources at the bottom.*

## The problem in numbers

Third-party delivery apps typically take **15–30% commission** per order,
with some plans and markups pushing higher. The cost lands on everyone
except the platform:

- **Restaurants** pay the commission, then mark up menu prices to survive
  it — so neighbors pay more for the same meal.
- **Drivers** net as little as a few dollars an hour after gas, tolls, and
  wear, with pay set by opaque algorithms.
- **Cities that capped fees** found platforms responding by de-prioritizing
  capped local spots in search results in favor of chains on unrestricted
  ad tiers. Caps alone don't fix the power imbalance.

The core issue isn't delivery logistics — it's who owns the rails. When a
distant platform owns ordering and dispatch, it taxes every meal. A
community that owns its own rails can run the same coordination at cost.

## Models that already work

- **Co-op bulk buying and box delivery.** Members order together from farms
  and wholesalers; the co-op delivers on a schedule. Surplus is reinvested
  or returned as lower prices. Research finds these co-ops reduce food
  insecurity and improve diets.
- **Mutual-aid food projects.** Volunteer-run networks pack and deliver
  grocery bags and hot meals on solidarity, not charity — horizontal,
  neighbor-to-neighbor. Several run volunteer driver networks today.
- **Community fridges and meal trains.** Low-tech, high-trust: stocked
  fridges open round the clock, organized meal deliveries for new parents,
  illness, and hardship.
- **In-house restaurant delivery.** Restaurants that run their own delivery
  keep the margin and the customer relationship, but take on drivers,
  insurance, and scheduling themselves — exactly the burden a shared
  community service could pool.

## The Strongo approach

1. **Zero commission.** Restaurants and home cooks list at their real
   prices. Delivery covers driver stipends and operating costs only.
2. **Drivers keep full pay plus tips.** Volunteer drivers earn stipends set
   openly to cover transportation and time — no algorithmic cuts.
3. **Bulk ordering built in.** Weekly co-op style bulk orders from regional
   vendors sit next to per-meal delivery, so the service serves both daily
   dinners and grocery runs.
4. **Care rails from day one.** The same dispatch handles free-meal funds,
   meal trains, and fridge restocking — care for people in need isn't a
   separate program, it's a delivery priority.
5. **Open books.** Fees, stipends, and surplus are published. Surplus funds
   free meals and the next service, decided in the open.

## What could go wrong

- **Food safety and liability.** Handling, storage, and allergen rules need
  real answers before hot meals scale. Start narrow (shelf-stable bulk,
  partner restaurants with their own licenses) and expand deliberately.
- **Driver coverage.** Volunteer networks have gaps. Stipends, scheduled
  shifts, and honest coverage maps beat pretending to be always-on.
- **Insurance.** Delivery driving needs coverage for drivers and the
  organization. Price this before the pilot, not after.
- **Scale vs. trust.** The model works because it's local. Growth means
  replicating per-neighborhood chapters, not one giant dispatch pool.

## Open questions

- Pilot scope: one neighborhood, how many restaurants, which days?
- Stipend levels and funding for the first 8 weeks.
- Ordering channel for the pilot: simple web form, shared spreadsheet, or
  Strongo v1 ordering?
